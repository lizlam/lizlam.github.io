Powers of 2

Computers love doubling!

Every computer uses binary — numbers made only of 0s and 1s. Each position is a power of 2: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32…

The rice & chessboard story

A wise man asked a king for 1 grain of rice on square 1 of a chessboard, 2 on square 2, 4 on square 3, doubling each time. By square 64 the king owed more rice than exists in the world!

That's how fast powers of 2 grow: 2⁰=1, 2¹=2, 2²=4, 2³=8, 2⁴=16…

Try it yourself and put grains of rice on the bottom row of a chessboard.

Take a some grains of rice and put 1 grain in the lower right square.

Put 2 (doubling 1) in the next square to the left.

Continue as best you can and see if you can finish the row.

Card version

  • Take cards numbered 1, 2, 4, 8 (or write these numbers on blank cards).
  • Pick any number from 1 to 15 (e.g., 13).
  • Make it by adding the right cards: 8 + 4 + 1 = 13.
  • That's exactly how computers store numbers in binary!

Challenge: Can you make 10? (8 + 2)

Mission: From Rice Grains to Binary Bits

Imagine the bottom row of your chessboard is a row of 8 light switches. Each switch controls a power of 2:

128 64 32 16 8 4 2 1
2⁷2⁶2⁵2⁴2³2²2¹2⁰

Rightmost square = 1 (smallest). Leftmost = 128 (biggest).

Your goal: "Turn on" some squares by placing a bean / coin / card that says "ON" (or just imagine a glowing light!). The total number is what you get by adding the values of the ON squares.

  • Make the number 13. Which lights do you turn on? → 8 + 4 + 1
  • Make 25. (16 + 8 + 1)
  • Make 100. (64 + 32 + 4)
  • Challenge: What is the biggest number you can make with only 4 lights on? (Try it!)
  • Super challenge: Can you make 255? Which lights? (All of them!)

Think: Why do computers use exactly this system? Because each "light" is just a bit — on (1) or off (0)!
